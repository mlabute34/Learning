What competent means here

After these 40 hours you can design, build and debug a multi-task ESP32 application: tasks with deliberate priorities and measured stack sizes, data moving through queues instead of shared globals, interrupts that hand work to tasks, timing you have verified with a logic analyzer, and crashes you can trace to a line of code.

That is the level of a solid junior firmware developer. Depth beyond that comes from the next few projects you ship; the last section points at them.

How to work through it

Sessions run 1 to 2.5 hours. Do them in order, since later work reuses earlier code: the ring buffer you write in session 1.3 ends up inside the capstone.

Finish every project before moving on. A project left at 80% teaches far less than a finished one, because the last 20% is where the bugs live.

Keep one git repository with a folder per session, and a lab notebook where you write what you expected and what you measured. Type code out rather than pasting it.

Versions. This course targets ESP-IDF v6.1, the current stable release. Version 6 removed several legacy drivers and moved MQTT and cJSON out into the component registry, so many older tutorials no longer compile. Set the docs version selector to your version and chip every time. Warnings are treated as errors by default in v6; fix them rather than switching that off.

Kit

ESP32-WROOM-32E dev board (ESP32S or DevKitC style)The classic dual-core ESP32. On Windows you may need the CP210x or CH340 USB driver, depending on the chip beside the USB port.
DRV8825 driver carrier and a NEMA 17 bipolar stepperPlus a 12 V supply rated above your motor current, and a 100 µF electrolytic capacitor across VMOT and GND, right at the driver.
10 kΩ NTC thermistor and a 10 kΩ resistorThermal protection. Note the B value from its listing; 3950 is common.
Photoresistor, 10 kΩ resistor and an LEDThe LED shines on the photoresistor and a flag on the motor shaft breaks the beam, which makes a homing sensor.
Breadboard, jumpers, 2 buttons, 10 kΩ pot, 2 LEDs with 330 Ω resistors, a few 0.1 µF capacitorsThe capacitors go from each analog input to ground to steady the readings.
MultimeterRequired for setting the driver's current limit.
24 MHz 8-channel USB logic analyzerUsed with PulseView, which is free. The most valuable $15 in the kit.

Before the motor ever moves. Set the DRV8825 current limit with a multimeter before connecting the motor. On Pololu's carrier the sense resistors are 0.100 Ω, so the current limit is 2 × VREF; check the resistors on a clone board. In full-step mode the coils get about 70% of that limit. Without a heatsink, stay at or below about 1.5 A. Never connect or disconnect the motor while the driver is powered, and join the ESP32's ground to the driver's ground.

Suggested pin map

STEP, DIR, ENABLEGPIO25, GPIO26, GPIO27. Add a 10 kΩ pull-up from ENABLE to 3.3 V so the driver stays disabled while the ESP32 boots.
FAULTGPIO33, with the internal pull-up enabled.
RESET and SLEEPBoth to 3.3 V, as in Pololu's minimal wiring diagram. Leave MODE0 to MODE2 unconnected for full step while you learn.
Thermistor, photoresistor, potGPIO36, GPIO39, GPIO34. All on ADC1, all input-only.
ButtonsGPIO18 and GPIO19, with internal pull-ups.
Status LEDGPIO23, or the onboard LED, which is usually GPIO2.

The rules behind that map: never use GPIO6 to 11, which connect to the module's flash. Keep GPIO0, 2, 5, 12 and 15 free of anything that pulls them at boot; GPIO12 in particular can stop the board booting. GPIO34 to 39 are input-only with no internal pull-ups. Analog inputs go on ADC1 (GPIO32 to 39), because ADC2 stops working while Wi-Fi is on.

2.2First steps: the DRV8825 on a logic analyzer

1.5 h
Watch

Tasks and the scheduler

Create tasks on purpose, with the right priority, stack and delay, and prove their timing.

4.1Tasks and priorities

1.5 h
Watch
Introduction to RTOS, Part 2 (Getting Started with FreeRTOS) and Part 3 (Task Scheduling).
Read
FreeRTOS book: Task Management, up to and including the idle task. Then the ESP-IDF API Reference page FreeRTOS (IDF): its overview and the differences from vanilla FreeRTOS.
Do
  • Create two LED tasks at the same priority with different blink rates, plus a third task that logs a heartbeat. Stack sizes are in bytes.

Nothing should change, because both tasks spend nearly all their time blocked. Make sure you can explain why.

4.2Delays that don't drift

1 h
Read
FreeRTOS book: the Blocked state, vTaskDelay and the delay-until sections of Task Management. In current code use xTaskDelayUntil, which also reports whether you missed the deadline.
Do

Write a task that toggles a pin every 50 ms, but does 0 to 20 ms of random busy work each cycle using esp_random and esp_rom_delay_us. Capture 10 seconds on the analyzer.

With vTaskDelay the period wanders. Switch to xTaskDelayUntil and it locks to 50 ms. Record both measurements in your notebook.

4.3Stacks, starvation and visibility

1.5 h
Watch
Introduction to RTOS, Part 4 (Memory Management).
Read
Skim the FreeRTOS book's Heap Memory Management chapter for concepts only, since ESP-IDF uses its own heap implementation. In the ESP-IDF FreeRTOS docs, find vTaskList and vTaskGetRunTimeStats and the menuconfig options they need.
Do
  • Log uxTaskGetStackHighWaterMark for each task. Confirm stack overflow checking is enabled in menuconfig.

  • Add a high-priority task that busy-loops and never blocks. Watch lower-priority LEDs freeze on the analyzer and the task watchdog complain.
  • Print the task list and run-time stats before and after, then fix the task by making it block.

7.2Analog sensing you can trust

1.5 h
Read
The ADC Oneshot Mode page again, focusing on calibration and noise. Your thermistor's listing for its resistance at 25 °C and its B value, and the Beta equation: 1/T = 1/T₀ + ln(R/R₀)/B, with temperatures in kelvin.
Do
  • Design the thermistor divider so its voltage stays inside the calibrated ADC range (roughly 150 to 2450 mV at 12 dB on the classic ESP32) across the temperatures you care about. Put the math in your notebook.
  • Build a Thermistor class that turns calibrated millivolts into resistance, then °C, averaged through your RingBuffer. Check it against a known reference, such as room temperature on a thermometer or ice water with the thermistor sealed in a bag.
  • Measure the photoresistor's response time in both directions by logging esp_timer_get_time as you block and unblock the beam. The slower direction limits how fast you can home.
  • Turn the photoresistor into a clean beam-blocked signal with two thresholds, so noise near a single threshold can't make it chatter.

7.4Break it on purpose

1 h
Read
API Guides: Fatal Errors; Watchdogs.
Do

Cause and diagnose four failures, writing down each symptom and how you recognized it:

  • A stack overflow.
  • A write through a null pointer. The panic will name it StoreProhibited.
  • A starved idle task, caught by the task watchdog.
  • A deadlock. There is no panic at all, and only the task list reveals it.

Let idf.py monitor decode each backtrace to a file and line. The classic ESP32 has no built-in USB JTAG, so stepping through code in a debugger needs an external adapter such as the ESP-Prog on GPIO12 to 15. Leave that for later.

Capstone: stepper controller with homing and thermal protection

One finished device that uses everything above, with a README that proves it meets its spec.

The spec

  1. A GPTimer alarm callback generates STEP pulses that meet the DRV8825's timing. Moves follow a trapezoidal profile, accelerating, cruising and decelerating to a target position in steps. Step intervals are precomputed as integers, with no floating point inside the interrupt.
  2. On boot the controller homes to the optical endstop: approach fast, back off, approach slowly. Position 0 is the beam edge, and failing to find it within a set travel is a fault.
  3. The thermistor sits on the motor body or driver heatsink, sampled at 10 Hz with xTaskDelayUntil and smoothed with your RingBuffer. Above a warning temperature, max speed halves. Above a limit, the driver is disabled until it cools below a lower threshold.
  4. The DRV8825 FAULT line goes through an ISR and a notification, stopping motion immediately and entering the fault state.
  5. An explicit state machine with HOMING, IDLE, MOVING and FAULT states, plus a derated flag, logs every transition.
  6. The pot selects a target position, read at 10 Hz. A short button press moves there, and holding for 2 seconds re-homes.
  7. Over MQTT, publish state, position and temperature every second, and accept move and home commands that feed the same command queue as the button. Losing Wi-Fi never disturbs motion or protection.
  8. One gatekeeper task owns logging. A health task logs every task's stack high-water mark, free heap and minimum-ever free heap every 30 seconds.

Rules: no busy-waiting, no unprotected shared state, exactly one owner for the position counter, no heap allocation of your own after startup, and every esp_err_t checked.

8.4Acceptance testing

1 h
Do

Run each test and record the results in your README:

  • Capture STEP and DIR through a full move while the controller publishes. Confirm the ramp shape and minimum pulse widths, with no gaps in the pulse train.
  • Home 10 times from different positions. After each, move to the same position and record the worst difference you see.
  • Warm the thermistor with your fingers or warm air until it crosses both thresholds. Confirm derating, cutoff, and recovery only below the lower threshold.
  • Simulate a driver fault by pulling the FAULT line low with a button. Confirm motion stops within one step period.
  • Kill the network for 5 minutes, for example by switching off a phone hotspot. Moves and protection carry on, and MQTT reconnects on its own.
  • Run back-and-forth moves for an hour, then home and compare the step count at the beam edge with the expected value to detect missed steps. Minimum free heap should level off.

Finish the README with both diagrams, your measurements, and what you would change in a second version.
